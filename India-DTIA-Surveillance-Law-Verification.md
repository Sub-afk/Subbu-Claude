# India: verification of the DTIA surveillance paragraph (status as of 28 October 2026)

**Context:** This is a Data Transfer Impact Assessment (DTIA). An EU-based law firm (the data exporter) transfers personal data to Indian vendors (the data importers), and the paragraph under review sets out the Indian surveillance-law risk analysis.
**Task:** Check each law, rule, case and factual statement in the paragraph against the current position. For each one, say whether it is still in force, has been amended or replaced, or has been repealed, and whether the paragraph describes it correctly.
**Method:** I relied on primary sources first: the Gazette of India, India Code, ministry and regulator websites (MeitY, DoT, CERT-In, MHA) and the court judgments themselves. Secondary sources (law-firm notes, legal news, NGOs) are used only where no primary source could be retrieved, and each one is labelled **[S]**. Primary sources are labelled **[P]**.
**Cut-off:** 28 October 2026.

**Status legend:** ✅ Accurate and current · ⚠️ Partly accurate, imprecise, or outdated in part · ❌ Inaccurate or superseded

## 2. Section 1 of the paragraph: the general risk statement

### 2.1 "Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act of 1978 orders" — ❌ wrong jurisdiction

- **Finding:** FISA (50 U.S.C. §1801 et seq.) is United States law. Indian government authorities do not issue FISA orders. An Indian company is exposed to FISA s.702 only if it is a US "electronic communication service provider" (ECSP), or if EU data passes through a US provider it uses (for example Microsoft 365, Google Workspace or AWS). In that case the onward-transfer risk is US risk, not Indian risk.
- **Current status of s.702 (for completeness):** The Reforming Intelligence and Securing America Act 2024 (RISAA) reauthorised s.702 until **20 Apr 2026**. Congress then passed short extensions (a 10-day patch, followed by a 45-day extension on 30 Apr 2026). Advocacy and Congressional sources report that the statutory authority **lapsed in mid-June 2026**. However, the FISC certification of **17 Mar 2026** and the directives issued under it are grandfathered until they expire (about March 2027). I could not confirm any reauthorisation between mid-September and 28 Oct 2026. **Check congress.gov before finalising.**
- **Suggested correction:** Remove the FISA reference from the Indian analysis. Instead, add a sub-processor note: if the importer uses US-based providers, the US analysis under FISA s.702, EO 12333 and EO 14086 / the EU–US Data Privacy Framework applies to those flows.
- **Sources:**
  - [S] Congressional Research Service, *FISA Section 702 and the 2024 RISAA* — https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R48592
  - [S] NPR, "Congress extends FISA 702 surveillance program for 45 days" (29 Apr 2026) — https://www.npr.org/2026/04/29/g-s1-119094/congress-fisa-702
  - [S] Brennan Center, *Section 702: 2026 Resource Page* (updated 17 Sep 2026) — https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/section-702-foreign-intelligence-surveillance-act-fisa-2026-resource-page

### 2.2 Cooperation with EU and UK agencies — ✅ (a risk statement, not a legal claim)
This is a reasonable risk statement and needs no legal verification. Note that India is not a member of the Five Eyes arrangement. Formal law-enforcement cooperation runs through MLATs and letters rogatory (for example the India–UK MLAT of 1992). No source was located showing structured signals-intelligence sharing between India and the EU.

## 3. Section 2 of the paragraph: the specific laws

### 3.1 Information Technology Act 2000, s.69 (interception, monitoring, decryption) — ✅ with corrections

- **Status:** **In force.** The section was substituted by the IT (Amendment) Act 2008 (Act 10 of 2009) with effect from **27 Oct 2009**, and has not been amended since. The Jan Vishwas (Amendment of Provisions) Act 2026 (Act 8 of 2026, assent 7 Apr 2026) does **not** amend the IT Act: a text search of the Act found no reference to it. The proposed "Digital India Act" that would replace the IT Act has **not** been introduced in Parliament.
- **Verified text (s.69(1)):** The power may be exercised by "the Central Government or a State Government or any of its officers specially authorised". The grounds are "sovereignty or integrity of India, defence of India, security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States or public order or for preventing incitement to the commission of any cognizable offence relating to above or for investigation of any offence". Reasons must be recorded in writing. The paragraph's summary is accurate.
- **Correction (penalties and duty-bearers):** Under **s.69(3)**, the duty to assist falls on the **"subscriber or intermediary or any person in-charge of the computer resource"**. That includes the law-firm importer itself, for example where it is required to decrypt. Under **s.69(4)**, failure to assist is punishable with **up to 7 years' imprisonment and a fine**. The paragraph merges this with s.69B, where the penalty is **up to 3 years and a fine, and applies to intermediaries only** (s.69B(4)).
- **Pending challenge:** Constitutional challenges to s.69 and to the MHA order of 20 Dec 2018, filed by Internet Freedom Foundation, Manohar Lal Sharma and others, have been pending in the Supreme Court since the court issued notice in Jan 2019. No interim stay was granted. No final decision was located.

### 3.2 IT Act s.69B (traffic data) — ✅ with corrections

- **Status:** **In force** (inserted with effect from 27 Oct 2009, not amended since).
- **Corrections:**
  - The statutory wording is "to enhance cyber security and for identification, analysis and prevention of intrusion or spread of **computer contaminant**". The paragraph says "computer containment".
  - The definition of "traffic data" in the Explanation is quoted correctly.
  - The power belongs to the **Central Government only**, and is exercised by **notification in the Official Gazette** authorising an agency.
  - The sentence "the intermediary or persons in charge… are required to extend all facilities… to facilitate such interception, monitoring or decryption, or face imprisonment" combines s.69(3)–(4) with s.69B(2) and (4). They should be stated separately (see §3.1).
- **Source:** [P] IT Act 2000, s.69B (India Code link above).

### 3.3 IT Act s.75 (extraterritorial application) — ✅

- **Status:** **In force and unchanged.** The paragraph is accurate. Under s.75(2), the Act applies to offences committed outside India "if the act or conduct constituting the offence or contravention involves a computer, computer system or computer network located in India".
- **Source:** [P] IT Act 2000, s.75 (India Code link above).

### 3.4 IT Interception Rules 2009 and IT Monitoring Rules 2009 — ⚠️

- **Interception Rules:** Information Technology (Procedure and Safeguards for Interception, Monitoring and Decryption of Information) Rules 2009, **G.S.R. 780(E), 27 Oct 2009**, made under s.69(2) read with s.87(2)(y). **In force.**
  - **Amendment not mentioned in the paragraph:** the **IT (Procedure and Safeguards for Interception, Monitoring and Decryption of Information) Amendment Rules 2024, dated 26 Feb 2024**. These amend **rule 23(1)** so that "competent authority and the security agency" (previously only "security agency") must destroy interception records every six months. Commentators criticised the change as reducing transparency and oversight.
- **Monitoring Rules:** Information Technology (Procedure and Safeguards for Monitoring and Collecting Traffic Data or Information) Rules 2009, **G.S.R. 782(E), 27 Oct 2009**, made under **s.69B(3)**. **In force.**
- **Correction:** The paragraph says both sets of rules "set out the procedural law for any cyber surveillance conducted under Section 69". In fact the Interception Rules implement **s.69** and the Monitoring Rules implement **s.69B**.
- **Status of the 2009 IT rules versus the 2024 Telecom rules:** The Telecom Interception Rules 2024 supersede rules 419 and 419A of the **Telegraph** Rules only. The **IT Act** interception rules are a separate regime and remain in force. In practice there are now two parallel regimes: IT Act s.69 for data in a "computer resource", and Telecom Act s.20(2) for "messages" carried by telecom services and networks.

### 3.5 SPDI Rules 2011, rule 6 — ⚠️ (in force, but with a defined end date)

- **Instrument:** Information Technology (Reasonable Security Practices and Procedures and Sensitive Personal Data or Information) Rules 2011, **G.S.R. 313(E), 11 Apr 2011**, made under s.87(2)(ob) read with **s.43A** of the IT Act.
- **Status:** **In force today.** DPDP Act **s.44(2)(a) omits IT Act s.43A** and s.44(2)(c) omits s.87(2)(ob), but under commencement notification **G.S.R. 843(E)** s.44(2) commences only on **13 May 2027** (18 months from 13 Nov 2025). Until that date, s.43A and the SPDI Rules continue to apply. The paragraph's phrase "DPDP, once notified, will replace the SPDI rules" should now give that date.
- **Verified text and corrections:**
  - The government-sharing exception is in the **proviso to rule 6(1)**. It applies to "Government agencies mandated under the law to obtain information… for the purpose of verification of identity, or for prevention, detection, investigation including cyber incidents, prosecution, and punishment of offences". The agency must send a written request "stating clearly the purpose".
  - The rule says the agency "shall **also state** that the information so obtained shall not be published or shared with any other person". The paragraph's "shall not be allowed to share" overstates this. It is a required statement in the request, not a separate prohibition with any enforcement mechanism.
  - The rules use the term **"body corporate"**, not "data controller or data processor". Under the s.43A Explanation, "body corporate" includes a firm or other association "engaged in commercial or professional activities", which covers a law firm.
  - **Rule 6(2)** (disclosure to a third party by "an order under the law for the time being in force") is summarised correctly.
- **Point the paragraph misses:** In its **Press Note of 24 Aug 2011**, MeitY clarified that a body corporate in India providing services under a contract with a legal entity located in or outside India is **not subject to rules 5 and 6**. An Indian importer acting as a contractual service provider to the EU firm is therefore generally outside rule 6 altogether. Government access in that situation would come through the general powers instead: IT Act s.69, BNSS s.94, Telecom Act s.20, and from 2027 DPDP s.36.

### 3.7 Telecom Act s.61 (existing rules continue) — ⚠️

### 3.8 Telecom Act s.20(2)(a) and the definitions — ⚠️

- **Status:** In force since 26 Jun 2024.
- **Verified text:** "On the occurrence of any public emergency or in the interest of public safety, the Central Government or a State Government or any officer specially authorised… may, if satisfied that it is necessary or expedient to do so, in the interest of the sovereignty and integrity of India, defence and security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States, public order, or for preventing incitement to the commission of any offence, subject to such procedure and safeguards as may be prescribed, and for reasons to be recorded in writing, by order — (a) direct that any message or class of messages, to or from any person or class of persons, to or from any telecommunication equipment or class of telecommunication equipment, or relating to any particular subject, brought for transmission by, or transmitted or received by any telecommunication service or telecommunication network, shall not be transmitted, or shall be intercepted or detained, or shall be **disclosed in intelligible format** to the officer mentioned in such order."
- **Corrections:**
  1. The power can be exercised only **"on the occurrence of any public emergency or in the interest of public safety"**. This is a threshold precondition (the PUCL reading of old s.5(2)), not one ground in a list of alternatives. The paragraph's first account is accurate; its second account blurs the point.
  2. "Disclosed in intelligible format" effectively obliges decryption or plain-text delivery. This is relevant to encrypted communications.
  3. Under **s.20(3)**, press messages of accredited correspondents may not be intercepted unless their transmission has been prohibited under s.20(2)(a).
  4. **Definitions:** the paragraph says "the definition of telecommunication is wide, and it includes any equipment, appliance, instrument…". That text is actually the definition of **"telecommunication equipment" in s.2(q)**, which also expressly includes "software and intelligence integral to such telecommunication equipment". **"Telecommunication" (s.2(p))** means "transmission, emission or reception of any messages, by wire, radio, optical or other electro-magnetic systems…". **"Message" (s.2(g))** includes "data stream, intelligence or information sent through telecommunication". **"Telecommunication service" (s.2(t))** is "any service for telecommunication", as the paragraph says.
- **Source:** [P] Telecommunications Act 2023, ss. 2 and 20 (links in §3.6). The quoted text was checked against the PRS copy of the Bill as introduced (Bill No. 194 of 2023), whose section numbering matches the enacted Act: https://prsindia.org/files/bills_acts/bills_parliament/2023/The%20Telecommunications%20Bill,%202023.pdf. Confirm against the Gazette copy before quoting.

### 3.9 Telecommunications (Procedures and Safeguards for Lawful Interception of Messages) Rules 2024 — ✅ / ⚠️ (amended)

- **Instrument:** **G.S.R. 754(E), 6 Dec 2024**, made under Telecom Act s.20(2) and (4) read with s.56. The draft was G.S.R. 522(E) of 28 Aug 2024. The paragraph's date is correct.
- **Supersession:** The rules are made in **supersession of rules 419 and 419A of the Indian Telegraph Rules 1951**. Interception orders already issued under those rules continue until they expire.
- **Key safeguards (relevant to the "lack of independent oversight" point):**
  - The **competent authority** is the Union Home Secretary, or the State Home Secretary. In "unavoidable circumstances" an officer of at least Joint Secretary rank may issue an order.
  - **In remote areas or for operational reasons**, the head or second-senior-most officer of the authorised agency (at State level, not below Inspector General of Police) may issue the order. It must be confirmed within **7 working days**. If it is not confirmed, interception ceases and the intercepted material must be destroyed within 2 working days. Commentators note this widens the old "emergent cases" exception in rule 419A.
  - Orders last **up to 60 days**, renewable to a **maximum of 180 days**.
  - **Review Committees** are made up of executive officers only: at the Centre, the Cabinet Secretary, the Law Secretary and the Telecom Secretary; in the States, the Chief Secretary and others. They meet **every two months**.
  - Records are destroyed every six months unless they are functionally required or a court directs otherwise.
  - There is **no judicial authorisation and no independent oversight body**. This supports the paragraph's later statement that India lacks independent oversight.
- **Amendment not mentioned in the paragraph:** **Telecommunications (Procedures and Safeguards for Lawful Interception of Messages) Amendment Rules 2025, G.S.R. 625(E), 12 Sep 2025** (draft G.S.R. 425(E), 27 Jun 2025), in force on publication:
  - It substitutes the definition of "competent authority" in rule 2(1)(c). The **Union Home Secretary** is now the competent authority where a **State Government requests interception beyond its territorial jurisdiction**.
  - It changes rule 4(1) from "two nodal officers" to "**one or more** nodal officers".

## 4. "Factors against" section: case law and factual claims

### 4.1 *People's Union for Civil Liberties v. Union of India* — ✅ with minor correction

- **Citation:** (1997) 1 SCC 301; AIR 1997 SC 568. Decided **18 Dec 1996** by Kuldip Singh and S. Saghir Ahmad JJ.
- **Verified:** Telephone tapping infringes **Article 21** (privacy) unless it follows a procedure established by law, and engages **Article 19(1)(a)** unless justified under Article 19(2). The Court laid down procedural safeguards, including Review Committees, pending rules under s.7(2)(b) of the Telegraph Act. The Court did note poor record-keeping by the authorities. The paragraph's framing, "in compliance with the restrictions… under Article 19(2)", is loose; the primary basis was Art. 21.
- **Follow-on rules:** **Rule 419A** of the Telegraph Rules (inserted in **2007**) codified the PUCL guidelines. The IT interception rules of 2009 followed the same model. **Update:** rule 419A was **superseded on 6 Dec 2024** by the Telecom Interception Rules 2024 (§3.9). The PUCL safeguards survive in substance in those rules.
- **Source:** [P] Supreme Court of India, judgment PDF — https://api.sci.gov.in/jonew/judis/14584.pdf

### 4.2 *Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India* — ✅

- **Citation:** (2017) 10 SCC 1. W.P.(C) 494 of 2012, nine-judge bench, **24 Aug 2017**. It held privacy to be a fundamental right under Art. 21 and Part III. The **proportionality** test (legality, legitimate aim, proportionality, and procedural safeguards) is set out in the plurality and concurring opinions. It was later applied in *Puttaswamy (Aadhaar)*, (2019) 1 SCC 1. The paragraph is accurate.
- **Source:** [P] Supreme Court of India, judgment PDF — https://api.sci.gov.in/supremecourt/2012/35071/35071_2012_Judgement_24-Aug-2017.pdf

### 4.3 *Vinit Kumar v. Central Bureau of Investigation* (Bom HC, 2019) — ✅ (spelling)

- **Verified:** Bombay High Court, **Criminal Writ Petition No. 2367 of 2019**, decided **22 Oct 2019** (Ranjit More and N.J. Jamdar JJ). The Court **quashed three MHA interception orders** (of 29 Oct 2009, 18 Dec 2009 and 24 Feb 2010) issued under Telegraph Act s.5(2), as not meeting the "public emergency / public safety" threshold and as violating PUCL, Puttaswamy and rule 419A. It **directed destruction** of the intercepted material. The paragraph is accurate, but the name is spelled "**Vinit**", not "Vineet". No reported reversal was located.
- **Source:** [P] Bombay High Court judgment (signed copy, hosted by Columbia Global Freedom of Expression) — https://globalfreedomofexpression.columbia.edu/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/Official-Judgment.pdf

### 4.4 Human Rights Watch, 26 Aug 2021 — ✅ with nuance

- **Verified:** HRW, with other organisations, published the joint statement "India: Spyware Use Violates Supreme Court Privacy Ruling" (26 Aug 2021). It states that "hacking is illegal under Indian law" (see IT Act ss. 43 and 66), that the government had not said whether it used Pegasus, and that the government argued in the Supreme Court that the cases had national-security implications. It cites reporting that **at least 300 Indian phone numbers** appeared on the leaked Pegasus Project list. Correction: these were *numbers on a leaked list*, not "people targeted".

### 4.5 Pegasus: *Manohar Lal Sharma v. Union of India* — ❌ factual error in the paragraph

### 4.6 Advocate–client privilege under the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam 2023 — ⚠️ needs update and qualification

- **Status:** BSA, Act No. 47 of 2023. **In force from 1 Jul 2024**. It repealed the Indian Evidence Act 1872. The paragraph is correct on this. Privilege is now in:
  - **s.132** (professional communications; formerly IEA s.126)
  - **s.133** (formerly s.128)
  - **s.134** (confidential communications with legal advisers; formerly s.129)
- **Major development:** ***In re: Summoning Advocates who give legal opinion or represent parties during investigation of cases and related issues*, Suo Motu W.P.(Crl.) No. 2 of 2025, Supreme Court, 31 Oct 2025** (B.R. Gavai CJI, K. Vinod Chandran and N.V. Anjaria JJ):
  - Investigating officers may summon an advocate only where one of the s.132 exceptions applies (for example, a communication in furtherance of an illegal purpose). The summons must specify the exception and requires the reasoned prior approval of an officer **not below Superintendent of Police**. It is subject to judicial review under BNSS s.528.
  - An advocate's **digital devices** may be ordered produced only **before the jurisdictional court**, not directly to the investigating officer. The advocate and client must be heard, and the examination must protect other clients' confidentiality.
  - **Documents** in the advocate's or client's possession are **not** covered by s.132 privilege.
  - **In-house counsel** are **not** "advocates" for s.132. They have only the limited s.134 protection.
- **Limits to state in the DTIA:**
  1. Privilege is an **evidentiary and testimonial protection**. It does **not** prevent executive interception or monitoring under IT Act s.69 or s.69B, or under Telecom Act s.20. Neither statute nor the interception rules contains a carve-out for legal communications.
  2. It attaches to **advocates** enrolled under the Advocates Act 1961. The importer's personnel must be advocates for s.132 to apply. Use "advocate–client privilege" rather than "attorney-client privilege".
  3. Data the importer holds that is not a privileged communication, such as client identity data, employee data or business-contact data, is not protected by privilege at all.
- **Sources:**

  - [P] Supreme Court judgment, 31 Oct 2025 (2025 LiveLaw (SC) 1051; copy hosted by LiveLaw) — https://www.livelaw.in/pdf_upload/2025-livelaw-sc-1051-in-re-summoning-advocates-31-oct-2025-631600.pdf
  - [S] SCC Online, "Supreme Court fortifies advocate-client privilege" (11 Nov 2025) — https://www.scconline.com/blog/post/2025/11/11/supreme-court-fortifies-advocate-client-privilege/
  - [S] Supreme Court Observer, judgment summary — https://www.scobserver.in/journal/summoning-advocates-who-represent-clients-under-investigation-judgement-summary/

### 4.7 Judicial review is available — ✅

- **Verified:** Under Art. 32 (Supreme Court) and Art. 226 (High Courts), anyone may seek a remedy for breach of Art. 21. **Art. 21 protects "any person", including non-citizens.** The "procedure established by law" and proportionality standards come from PUCL and Puttaswamy. *Vinit Kumar* (§4.3) shows the remedy working in practice. The paragraph is accurate.
- **Practical limit (supports the paragraph's own caveat):** Targets are not notified of interception. Interception records must be destroyed every six months (the 2024 Telecom Interception Rules and IT rule 23 as amended in 2024). Courts also defer on national-security disclosure (the Pegasus order of 29 Apr 2025). Together these make redress hard to obtain in practice. This matters for the EDPB "effective remedy" analysis.

### 4.8 Centre for Internet and Society recommendation — ⚠️ partly outdated

- The CIS call for **a privacy law to be enacted** has been overtaken: the DPDP Act was enacted in 2023 and its Rules notified in 2025.
- The CIS call to reform **surveillance law** in line with the "National Privacy Principles" (Justice A.P. Shah Group of Experts on Privacy, 2012) and the *International Principles on the Application of Human Rights to Communications Surveillance* (2013) **remains unaddressed**. No surveillance-reform legislation has been introduced.
- The final sentence of the paragraph ("…concern about the Act, especially over the potential for unfettered government surveillance that requires monitoring") does not say which Act it means. If it means the DPDP Act, cite s.17(2)(a) and s.36 / Rule 23 (§3.10). If it means the Telecom Act, cite s.20.
